# Vector quantization, product quantization, and learned codebooks

**Prior-art classification: established published methods. This note records prior art, not Quantum Morph Laboratory (QML) work. QML has not independently reproduced the methods or results summarized here.** Classical vector quantizer design, product quantization for approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search, and neural learned codebooks are related but distinct threads. The records below establish that all three mechanisms predate QML. [1] [2] [3] [4]

## Bibliographic record

Vector quantization (VQ) is a broad, mature family rather than a method introduced in one neural-model paper. A foundational codebook-design publication is **“An Algorithm for Vector Quantizer Design,”** by **Y. Linde, A. Buzo, and R. Gray**. It appeared in *IEEE Transactions on Communications*, volume 28, issue 1, pages 84–95, January 1980; DOI **10.1109/TCOM.1980.1094577**. The IEEE record and a bibliographic record confirm the publication and authors. [1] [13]

The seminal ANN paper considered here is **“Product Quantization for Nearest Neighbor Search,”** by **Hervé Jégou, Matthijs Douze, and Cordelia Schmid**. It appeared in *IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence*, volume 33, issue 1, pages 117–128, January 2011. DOI: **10.1109/TPAMI.2010.57**; the authors’ institutional deposit is HAL Id **inria-00514462**. [2] [3]

For neural representation learning, the primary paper is **“Neural Discrete Representation Learning,”** by **Aaron van den Oord, Oriol Vinyals, and Koray Kavukcuoglu**. It appeared in *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 30 (NIPS 2017)* and was published in the proceedings on December 4, 2017. The arXiv preprint was submitted November 2, 2017; the current arXiv record is v2, revised May 30, 2018. Identifier: **arXiv:1711.00937**; arXiv/DataCite DOI: **10.48550/arXiv.1711.00937**. [4] [5] [6]

## Mathematical principles

In ordinary VQ, a vector is assigned to one representative from a finite codebook. With squared Euclidean distortion, an assignment for input vector `x` and codebook `C={c₁,…,cₖ}` is `i*(x)=argminᵢ ||x−cᵢ||²`; the decoder/reconstructor uses `cᵢ*`. A codebook may be learned by clustering/training-sequence methods such as Lloyd-style iterative design. This makes the representation discrete, but the quality depends on codebook size, training distribution, and distortion objective. [1] [2]

**Product quantization (PQ)** makes a very large implicit codebook tractable by partitioning a `D`-dimensional vector into `M` subvectors of dimension `D/M`, then fitting a separate small codebook of `K` centroids to each subspace. Each subvector receives an index, and the whole code is the tuple of those indices. The Cartesian-product codebook has `Kᴹ` possible composite vectors, while storing only `M·K` subspace centroids. At `b=log₂K` bits per subquantizer, the code uses `M·b` bits. For ANN, asymmetric distance computation (ADC) leaves the query unquantized and sums precomputed query-to-centroid distances for the stored database code; symmetric distance computation (SDC) quantizes both sides. The paper reports ADC as the more accurate estimator in its tested settings. Its IVFADC variant adds a coarse inverted-file assignment and applies PQ to residuals, trading exhaustive scans for a shortlist scan. [2] [7]

**VQ-VAE** uses a learned embedding table as the codebook within a neural encoder-decoder. The encoder emits `zₑ(x)` and maps each vector to its nearest embedding `eₖ`, where `k=argminⱼ ||zₑ(x)−eⱼ||₂`. The decoder receives `zq(x)=eₖ`. Since hard nearest-neighbor assignment has no ordinary derivative, the authors use a straight-through estimator to copy decoder gradients to the encoder. Their loss combines reconstruction, codebook fitting, and encoder commitment terms: `L = reconstruction + ||sg[zₑ(x)]−e||²₂ + β||zₑ(x)−sg[e]||²₂`, where `sg` is stop-gradient. In the paper’s experiments the codebook is learned through the codebook-loss term; an exponential-moving-average alternative is discussed but was **not** used for those experiments. The initial prior is uniform during VQ-VAE training; after training, an autoregressive PixelCNN (images) or WaveNet (audio) prior is fit over the discrete codes. [5] [6]

## Paper-reported results

The figures below are **reported by the originating papers**, not independent replications by QML. The PQ numbers are from the Jégou et al. paper; FAISS documentation is an implementation reference, not the source of these research results. [2] [7]

For PQ, the paper tested SIFT descriptors (128 dimensions; 100,000 training vectors, one million database vectors, 10,000 queries) and GIST descriptors (960 dimensions; 100,000 training vectors, 1,000,991 database vectors, 500 queries). With 64-bit codes (`M=8`, 256 centroids per subquantizer), its GIST table reports: SDC, 16.8 ms and recall@100 0.446; ADC, 17.2 ms and recall@100 0.652; IVFADC with 1,024 coarse centroids and `w=8`, 8.8 ms and recall@100 0.682; IVFADC with 1,024 centroids and `w=64`, 65.9 ms and recall@100 0.744. The spectral-hashing comparator is 22.7 ms and recall@100 0.132. All methods in that table use 1,000,991 code comparisons except IVFADC, which compares a smaller number of candidates. The paper reports the timing on the same machine, and elsewhere reports comparisons using a single core; a specific processor model is not stated in the cited extract. These values are one paper’s experimental setup, not universal latency or quality guarantees. [2]

The PQ paper also compares against spectral hashing, Hamming embedding, and FLANN. For the SIFT and GIST recall curves it reports that PQ-based ADC/IVFADC outperforms spectral hashing, and that IVFADC outperforms Hamming embedding in the shown configurations. Against FLANN on SIFT, IVFADC is reported to have better search-quality/time trade-offs at many operating points; the paper reports an indexing structure under 25 MB versus over 250 MB for FLANN in that comparison. Its large-scale experiment extracted roughly two billion SIFT descriptors and compared IVFADC with Hamming embedding under matched scanned-memory and coarse-quantization conditions; the paper presents query-time scaling in a figure but the accessible text does not state a single exact billion-vector latency, so no exact value is asserted here. [2]

For VQ-VAE, the CIFAR-10 comparison used the same standard VAE architecture across models, trained for 250,000 steps with batch size 128 and Adam at learning rate `2e-4`. The authors report negative log-likelihood lower bounds of 4.51 bits/dimension for a continuous VAE, 4.67 for VQ-VAE, and 5.14 for VIMCO; lower is better. Thus these figures show VQ-VAE ahead of the tested discrete VIMCO baseline, but not better than the continuous VAE in this experiment. The paper gives 4.54 bits/dimension from a previously reported deep convolutional VAE as a contextual comparison. [5]

For ImageNet, the authors show a VQ-VAE encoding 128×128×3 images into a 32×32×1 latent grid with `K=512` entries, describing this as an approximately 42.6-fold reduction in raw bit count before modeling the latent prior. The reconstruction assessment is chiefly qualitative in the cited section. In speech experiments, they use VCTK speech from 109 speakers and a latent representation 64 times lower in temporal resolution; the paper describes content retention under reconstruction and speaker conversion qualitatively. For an unconditional speech prior, a separate 460-speaker dataset and 320 latent steps per 2.56-second chunk are reported. These latter findings are not presented as a standardized quantitative compression benchmark. [5]

## Computational requirements and limitations

PQ reduces code-storage and exhaustive-distance costs, but it still requires training subquantizers on representative vectors. Jégou et al. report separate 100,000-vector learning sets for SIFT and GIST. They describe PQ training as `M` k-means problems over `K` centroids in `D/M` dimensions; the coarse quantizer and inverted-file search add their own cost. Their article does not establish a universal hardware requirement, nor does the accessible experiment text identify the processor model. FAISS’s index documentation gives a practical code-size formula of `ceil(M·nbits/8)` bytes/vector for `IndexPQ` (plus identifiers/overheads for other index families), but this is software-maintainer documentation, not a paper benchmark. [2] [7]

PQ’s main structural limitation is the chosen subspace decomposition. It assumes independently quantized subspaces and may represent cross-subspace correlations poorly; the original paper discusses sensitivity to component ordering and notes that an automatic grouping method could improve results. The later **Optimized Product Quantization** paper explicitly notes dependence on prior knowledge of input structure and introduces learned/optimized transforms and subspace allocation to address it. Other tradeoffs include code length versus recall, training-data representativeness, and IVF search parameters versus latency/recall. [2] [12]

VQ-VAE’s straight-through gradient is an estimator rather than the true derivative of hard quantization. A finite codebook and nearest-neighbor assignment constrain what the latent representation can express; codebook-use health and commitment weight are practical concerns, although the initial paper describes its method as robust to `β` values 0.1–2.0 in its reported experiments. The authors leave jointly training the prior and VQ-VAE as future work, and note that a more perceptual image loss than pixel MSE could be explored. Audio and video claims in the paper are substantially qualitative. The paper gives reproducible task/architecture details for its CIFAR-10 comparison, but does not state a general GPU/CPU requirement or wall-clock training budget in the source sections consulted. [5]

The papers and software have separate rights. The arXiv record for VQ-VAE lists arXiv’s perpetual non-exclusive distribution license for the article; that is not an open-source license for code or weights. The IEEE PQ article is a scholarly publication, and the consulted IEEE/HAL records do not provide a permissive software license for the paper text or any weights. No general weight license for either paper was verified. Any actual model checkpoint or data artifact must be checked under its own terms. [3] [4]

- **FAISS** is a reputable Meta-maintained C++/Python library for similarity search and clustering. It implements PQ including k-means-trained product quantizers and indexes such as `IndexPQ` and `IndexIVFPQ`. The project declares **MIT License**. Its code/docs are an implementation of a family of methods, not proof that a particular QML experiment has reproduced the paper’s metrics. [7] [8] [9]
- **DeepMind Sonnet VQ-VAE module** is an author/organization-maintained reference implementation of VQ-VAE, with ordinary and EMA codebook update variants. Sonnet is licensed **Apache-2.0**; its source file identifies the method paper. No associated pretrained VQ-VAE weights or separate weight license were verified from the consulted repository pages. [10] [11]

## Relevance to QML and potential overlap

**Established published method:** VQ, PQ, and neural learned codebooks are established prior art for representing vectors with discrete indices and learned centroids. PQ is directly relevant to compact vector storage and approximate distance computation; VQ-VAE is relevant to learned discrete latent representations and neural encoders/decoders. These facts do not imply that QML originated either method or that any QML implementation is equivalent to them. **Independent reproduction:** QML has not reproduced these methods in the materials reviewed. [1] [2] [5]

**Potential overlap — QML hypothesis / unknown:** In the local index’s current RMQ description, RMQ proposes a shared latent structure with layer-specific transforms and residual information (`Wₗ ≈ Aₗ × S × Bₗ + Rₗ`). A learned codebook or product-structured code representation could be a comparator or component if RMQ quantizes shared factors or residuals; this is a conceptual adjacency, not evidence that RMQ is PQ or VQ. MAP is described locally as a proposed precision-allocation strategy based on functional importance/error propagation. It is adjacent at the broad topic of quantization and rate/quality allocation, but it is not the same objective as nearest-centroid assignment. QMF currently lacks a defined mathematical mechanism in the local index, so overlap is unknown. Acronym similarity is not evidence of technical identity or derivation. A definitive comparison requires the QML equations and implementation. [2] [5] [7]
