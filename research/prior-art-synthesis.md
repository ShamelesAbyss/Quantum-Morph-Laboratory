# Quantum Morph Laboratory — first-batch prior-art synthesis

## Scope and evidentiary limits

This synthesis compares only the completed first-batch records supplied for Quantum Morph Laboratory (QML), linked in the [prior-art index](prior-art-index.md). It does not add research or infer facts beyond those records. The sources range from peer-reviewed conference/journal papers to arXiv preprints, a technical article, project specifications, reference code, and maintainer documentation; these are not equivalent evidence. Numerical results below are therefore characterized as paper-reported or author/maintainer-reported as appropriate. **QML has not independently reproduced the methods or results in this batch.** The comparisons identify established precedents and useful axes for future experiments; they do not establish that any QML method is identical to, derived from, or novel over those precedents.

Most importantly, the supplied material does not provide complete formal definitions or implementations for RMQ, MAP, or QMF. Some records include working descriptions of RMQ as a possible shared latent structure with per-layer transforms and residuals, and MAP as a proposed functionally informed precision-allocation approach; these descriptions support only qualified comparison hypotheses. QMF remains insufficiently specified for a reliable method- or format-level comparison. Acronym similarity is not evidence of technical identity.

## What kinds of prior art are represented?

### 1. Containers and conventional quantization

GGUF is a tensor-and-metadata file format, while GGML’s Q4_0/Q4_1 are particular block quantization layouts and encoders. They should not be treated as one algorithm or as a scholarly “GGUF paper.” The recorded blocks group 32 values and amortize scale metadata (and, for Q4_1, an offset) over packed codes. This is a practical baseline for stored representation, type metadata, interoperability, and effective bits per value. The record also cites Jacob et al. (2018) as background on affine integer quantization and quantization-aware training, explicitly not as a description of GGUF or the GGML Q4 formats. The method/format distinction matters for any comparison of QML’s proposed representation with an on-disk container.

The recorded weaknesses are familiar but conditional: coarse quantization is lossy, extrema can affect block reconstruction, and requantizing an already quantized source can further reduce quality. Upstream llama.cpp documentation provides useful conversion and size examples, but its reported speed table lacks sufficient machine, backend, build, and command detail for independent reproduction. Thus these are documentation examples, not universal performance limits or controlled QML comparators.

### 2. Post-training quantization: different objectives, not a single baseline

The post-training methods in the batch share the broad goal of compressing existing weights without full retraining, but differ in what they preserve and how they choose codes:

- **GPTQ/OPTQ** minimizes calibration-activation-weighted layer output error and uses approximate second-order compensation to redistribute rounding error across weights. Its paper reports strong low-bit perplexity results for the tested LLMs, but also describes numerical stability measures, calibration dependence, and an OPT-66B outlier. Its C4 evaluation is not fully zero-shot because calibration data come from C4 training samples.
- **AWQ** uses calibration activations to estimate salient channels, applies an equivalent channel scaling, and then performs grouped low-bit weight quantization. The record reports results under specific model/task/bit settings and TinyChat system throughput; the latter combines weights with kernels and serving optimizations and therefore is not an algorithm-only speed claim. AWQ’s saliency rationale may be conceptually relevant to a MAP proposal, but it does not implement the QML ledger’s proposed cross-layer error propagation or adaptive allocation rule.
- **SmoothQuant** also uses an equivalent diagonal rescaling, but to migrate quantization difficulty between activations and weights for W8A8 execution. Its results depend on calibration statistics and alpha; the record includes degradation where static statistics diverge from evaluation activations. It is therefore a relevant reparameterization comparator, not evidence that low-bit weight-only schemes and W8A8 methods are interchangeable.
- **HQQ** is described in an author technical article rather than a verified formal proceedings/arXiv paper. It uses groupwise affine quantization and a half-quadratic solver for weight reconstruction without calibration. The author reports fast quantization and favorable memory/perplexity cases, while exact hardware/protocol details are missing and some 2-bit results show quality degradation. Since its objective is weight reconstruction rather than direct activation/output error, its figures cannot be read as a direct controlled comparison with GPTQ or AWQ.
- **QuIP and QuIP#** use calibration Hessians, quadratic proxy loss, rounding/error feedback, and transformations intended to improve incoherence. QuIP# further uses Hadamard transforms and lattice/vector codebooks, with optional fine-tuning. These papers provide important precedents for second-order error-aware rounding and low-bit vector representations. Their resource needs, calibration choices, and kernel caveats vary, and the original QuIP repository’s software license was not verified in the record.
- **AQLM** uses additive learned codebooks and calibration-aware reconstruction, with residual K-means initialization, beam search for discrete code assignments, and optimization of codebooks/scales. It is a strong comparator for learned multi-codebook representation and for code assignment formulated as a discrete MAP problem. The acronym “MAP” alone cannot imply QML method overlap: a feature-by-feature comparison of objective, factorization, inference and allocation rules is required.

These approaches expose recurring trade-offs. More calibration or optimization can improve the selected reconstruction proxy at added time and complexity. Weight-only methods do not directly quantize activations; W8A8 methods have different execution and kernel constraints. Group size, bitwidth, model architecture, calibration distribution, and hardware all influence results. A low nominal bit rate does not by itself determine full model size or task quality. Published comparisons frequently use different datasets, baselines, and implementations; the supplied records do not permit ranking these techniques as if measured in one common experiment.

### 3. Mixed precision: sensitivity proxies and hardware feedback

HAWQ and HAWQ-V2 estimate curvature-based sensitivity (top Hessian eigenvalue or Hessian trace) and combine it with quantization perturbation or a Pareto budget to choose bitwidths. HAQ instead frames layer precision selection as reinforcement learning with hardware/simulator feedback and validation accuracy reward. These are established, direct prior-art baselines for proposals that allocate precision by layer sensitivity or resource budget, and they are especially pertinent to the MAP working description.

However, these methods do not make allocation universal. HAWQ-V2’s trace estimates and Pareto score are proxies; HAWQ’s top-eigenvalue ranking requires manual bit choices; HAQ’s policy depends on the target hardware and incurs evaluation/fine-tuning cost. Reported benchmarks are vision-oriented and use specified accelerators, not QML’s Rust environment. The records lack a complete end-to-end search cost for HAQ and do not verify checkpoint licensing. Any QML comparison should match model, task, budget, quantizer, and hardware rather than compare headline values across papers.

### 4. Structured factors and low-rank/tensor representations

Another family reduces degrees of freedom by changing the structure of weights rather than merely assigning fewer bits to every original scalar:

- **TT/MPS** factors a tensor into a chain of cores. Unfolding ranks control storage, and TT-SVD has a stated Frobenius approximation guarantee. Separate established neural prior art applies TT to network weights. Rank growth, tensorization and mode ordering matter; dense-input TT-SVD requires the full tensor in memory, and lower parameter count does not guarantee a faster implementation.
- **Tensor Ring** changes the open chain to cyclic core contraction. The cited paper’s tested applications are synthetic signals, images and videos, not neural weight compression. Its reported outcomes therefore establish tensor approximation/feature-extraction evidence, not neural-model performance.
- **Tucker compression** factorizes selected convolution modes into a core and mode factors, uses VBMF for rank selection, and fine-tunes. The ICLR 2016 paper reports ImageNet and mobile results on its specified networks/devices; those results are not transferable guarantees. The paper itself notes that linear kernel reconstruction may cause accuracy loss and that practical speed depends on implementation.
- **Kronecker methods** express weights as products or sums of smaller factors, including generalized convolution decompositions and KroneckerBERT. Rank and factor shapes trade capacity against parameter/FLOP reduction. KroneckerBERT’s quality also depends on distillation and fine-tuning, so the effect cannot be attributed to factorization alone. Some code and weight licensing was not verified.
- **CompactifAI** applies SVD/MPO bond truncation to selected Llama 2 7B matrices, profiles layer sensitivity, then “heals” the compressed network. The single-model study reports different task losses, including a 5.31-point GSM8K drop for the 93% configuration. The labels and raw sizes are not fully reconciled by the stated denominator, so the record appropriately preserves the labels rather than deriving a new compression rate. Its core compression code and derived weights were not verified in the reviewed official repository.
- **CALDERA** approximates weights as a quantized full-shape backbone plus low-rank factors, optimizing activation-calibration-weighted error. It combines structure and precision rather than representing a pure tensor-network factorization or ordinary scalar PTQ. This makes it a close comparison point for a low-rank-plus-residual hypothesis, but the formulas provided are not identical: CALDERA’s stated Q is a quantized full-shape component, while the QML working sketch describes a shared latent structure with layer-specific transforms and a residual. Exact overlap remains unverified. Its paper’s approximation bound is not a downstream task guarantee, and its operative repository license could not be verified.

Across these methods, rank/bond dimension, topology, tensorization, and recovery training all create trade-offs between storage, approximation, optimization cost, and inference. CALDERA, CompactifAI, Tucker, and Kronecker records also show why “low rank” or “tensorized” is too broad a description to establish equivalence: core topology, what is shared, whether a dense/quantized backbone remains, the target objective, and how factors are applied all matter. The supplied records provide enough to identify these published families, but not to decide method identity with QML concepts.

### 5. Codebooks, discrete latents, and entropy coding

The VQ/PQ records establish mature precedents for assigning vectors to learned or clustered codebooks. Product quantization partitions a vector into subspaces and trades code length against approximation/search quality; its fixed subspace partition can miss cross-subspace correlations. VQ-VAE learns discrete latent embeddings with a straight-through estimator, but is a learned representation method rather than evidence of neural-weight compression. AQLM shares the codebook theme while targeting neural weights with calibration-aware reconstruction. The distinctions between nearest-centroid assignment, product subspaces, additive codebooks, and neural discrete latents should be preserved.

Learned image-compression work establishes another distinct line: rate-distortion objectives, entropy models, hyperprior side information, autoregressive context, and arithmetic coding. Its distortion target is image reconstruction, not a neural network’s downstream function. Autoregressive decoding is serial, and some headline comparisons use entropy estimates rather than full bitstream decoding. These works may inform how a future QML format handles code lengths and side information, but do not establish that QML’s model representation uses an equivalent entropy model or objective.

### 6. Train-from-scratch low-bit networks

BitNet and BitNet b1.58 are not ordinary post-training quantizers. They train low-bit Transformer linear layers from scratch using sign or ternary weights, straight-through estimation, and high-precision latent weights/gradients/optimizer state; reported inference benefits rely on specialized implementation. The alphabet size of three symbols implies approximately 1.585 information bits per symbol, but not necessarily 1.585 physical bits per stored weight after packing, scaling, and metadata. The records explicitly note non-low-bit components and distinguish measured/maintainer hardware results from modeled energy estimates. This is relevant prior art for the broad question of neural representation, but not a drop-in conversion baseline for arbitrary pretrained models.

## Established baselines and practical comparison axes

Taken together, the batch supports a baseline map rather than a winner:

1. **File and scalar/block representation:** GGUF/GGML block types; report effective bits including scales/offsets, model artifact size, reader compatibility, and quality impact separately.
2. **Calibration-based post-training weight quantization:** GPTQ/OPTQ, AWQ, HQQ, QuIP/QuIP#, AQLM; report the objective, calibration data, group/codebook structure, quantization time, bit accounting, and task metrics.
3. **Activation quantization/reparameterization:** SmoothQuant and relevant AWQ mechanisms; distinguish W8A8 or activation-aware execution from weight-only compression.
4. **Mixed-precision allocation:** HAWQ/HAWQ-V2 and HAQ; compare sensitivity estimator, budget, target hardware, search cost, and whether activation bits are included.
5. **Structured parameterization:** TT/MPS, TR, Tucker, Kronecker, MPO, and CALDERA; report factor topology, what is shared, rank selection, residual/backbone representation, recovery training, and actual inference kernels.
6. **Discrete codebooks and coding:** VQ/PQ, AQLM, and learned entropy coding; distinguish search/latent objectives from neural-weight functional preservation and account for codebook/side-information overhead.
7. **Train-from-scratch alternatives:** BitNet; distinguish training protocol and high-precision state from post-training conversion.

Useful common evaluation axes emerge from the records: complete artifact size and bits per parameter including overhead; weight and activation precision; reconstruction/perplexity/task quality under matched calibration and evaluation data; encode/calibration cost and peak memory; inference throughput/latency, memory, and energy with hardware/backend/build specified; and compatibility and license status for code, dependencies, data, base weights, and derived checkpoints. The supplied papers do not collectively report all these metrics, and their performance tables should not be conflated.

## Missing coverage and limitations

This is a curated first batch, not comprehensive prior-art or patent research. It does not establish coverage of all scalar/vector quantizers, mixed-precision and sensitivity methods, pruning, distillation, tensor-network variants, low-rank methods, learned codecs, model formats, or relevant patents. The records do not include a systematic search protocol or full claim-by-claim comparison against QML’s proposed designs. In particular, exact RMQ/MAP/QMF equations, implementations, and intended claims are needed before method-level overlap can be evaluated. No conclusion about novelty, priority, freedom to operate, or non-infringement follows from this synthesis.

Evidence is also limited by what each source reports. Many results are author-reported, some are maintainer documentation, and timings often omit hardware/software detail or combine algorithms with optimized kernels. Some papers report proxy losses, selected benchmarks, or estimates rather than complete task/function measurements. An implementation repository is not independent reproduction. No QML reproduction is reported for any method in this batch.

Finally, licensing remains artifact-specific and incomplete. MIT/Apache/BSD code licenses cited for some implementations do not license model weights, datasets, dependencies, or papers. The records explicitly leave unresolved (among others) the GGUF parser-package license, QuIP’s original repository license, CALDERA’s operative code license, CompactifAI core implementation and derived-weight terms, and multiple checkpoint/base-model licenses. These uncertainties should be checked at the artifact level before reuse or redistribution.
