# Mixed-precision and sensitivity/activation-aware precision allocation

**Classification:** established published prior art. This note documents methods published by external research groups; it is not QML work and does not imply originality by Quantum Morph Laboratory. QML has not independently reproduced these methods, as specified for this index entry.

## Scope and canonical papers

The topic is a family of methods rather than one single algorithm. Three directly relevant, established examples are HAWQ, HAWQ-V2, and HAQ. HAWQ and its successor HAWQ-V2 use Hessian-derived sensitivity to guide layerwise precision. HAQ uses reinforcement learning (RL) and feedback from a target-hardware simulator to select weight and activation bitwidths. These papers establish that adaptive precision assignment predates QML; they do not establish any particular QML implementation or claimed overlap beyond the conceptual mechanisms described here. [1] [3] [5]

**HAWQ** is “HAWQ: Hessian AWare Quantization of Neural Networks With Mixed-Precision,” by Zhen Dong, Zhewei Yao, Amir Gholami, Michael W. Mahoney, and Kurt Keutzer. It appeared in the Proceedings of the IEEE/CVF International Conference on Computer Vision (ICCV), 2019, pp. 293–302; the arXiv preprint was submitted 29 April 2019. The ICCV proceedings page is the canonical publication record. The arXiv identifier is 1905.03696; its arXiv DOI is 10.48550/arXiv.1905.03696. No separate DOI for the ICCV proceedings article was verified in the cited records. [1] [2]

**HAWQ-V2** is “HAWQ-V2: Hessian Aware trace-Weighted Quantization of Neural Networks,” by Zhen Dong, Zhewei Yao, Yaohui Cai, Daiyaan Arfeen, Amir Gholami, Michael W. Mahoney, and Kurt Keutzer. It was published in *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 33* (NeurIPS 2020). Its arXiv preprint, 1911.03852, was submitted 10 November 2019; the arXiv DOI is 10.48550/arXiv.1911.03852. The NeurIPS proceedings page is the canonical publication record. No separate proceedings DOI was verified here. [3] [4]

**HAQ** is “HAQ: Hardware-Aware Automated Quantization With Mixed Precision,” by Kuan Wang, Zhijian Liu, Yujun Lin, Ji Lin, and Song Han. It appeared in the Proceedings of the IEEE/CVF Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR), 2019, pp. 8612–8620; the arXiv preprint was submitted 21 November 2018 and revised 6 April 2019. The CVPR proceedings page is the canonical publication record. The arXiv identifier is 1811.08886; its arXiv DOI is 10.48550/arXiv.1811.08886. No separate DOI for the CVPR proceedings article was verified in the cited records. [5] [6]

## Mathematical principles and mechanisms

A quantizer maps scalar or tensor values to one of a finite number of representable levels; bitwidth determines the number of levels. Mixed precision assigns different bitwidths to different layers and, in some methods, separately to weights and activations. The allocation problem trades quantization error and model quality against a budget such as model size, latency, energy, or activation memory. [2] [4] [6]

HAWQ approximates the local loss change from perturbing a layer’s weights with a second-order Taylor expansion. If a layer/block has Hessian top eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\), HAWQ uses \(S_i=\lambda_i/n_i\), where \(n_i\) is the block parameter count, to rank relative sensitivity while accounting for parameter footprint. Higher-ranked/sensitive blocks receive relatively more precision. It also orders staged quantization-aware fine-tuning by \(\Omega_i=\lambda_i\|Q(W_i)-W_i\|_2^2\), prioritizing blocks whose curvature and quantization perturbation jointly predict larger loss impact. The method estimates Hessian-vector products without materializing the full Hessian. The original method provides a relative precision ranking rather than an absolute bit assignment. [2]

HAWQ-V2 replaces the top-eigenvalue-only sensitivity score with average Hessian trace, \(\overline{\mathrm{Tr}}(H_i)=\mathrm{Tr}(H_i)/n_i\). It estimates the trace matrix-free with Hutchinson’s identity: for random vectors \(z\) with identity covariance, \(\mathrm{Tr}(H)=\mathbb{E}[z^T H z]\), approximated by averaging several Hessian-vector products. For a candidate precision allocation, it scores second-order perturbation as \(\Omega=\sum_i \overline{\mathrm{Tr}}(H_i)\|Q(W_i)-W_i\|_2^2\). It first restricts candidates to allocations consistent with sensitivity ordering, then uses a Pareto frontier to select the minimum-\(\Omega\) configuration at a target model size. The authors state that this is a practical selection rule, not a theoretical guarantee of globally best post-quantization accuracy. [4]

HAWQ-V2 also extends curvature analysis to activations. It considers the Hessian with respect to a layer’s activations, not only weights. Because per-example activations are independent, the activation Hessian has block-diagonal structure by example; the authors exploit this to estimate trace by processing examples separately and averaging. The paper specifically reports activation sensitivity in object detection and uses Pareto selection for mixed-precision activations. This is distinct from merely calibrating activation ranges or quantizer clipping thresholds. [4]

HAQ is a complementary, hardware-conditioned allocator, not a Hessian-sensitivity metric. Its DDPG actor-critic agent proceeds layer by layer. The state includes layer index, dimensions, kernel/stride/feature-map information, parameter count, depthwise-convolution indicator, whether the decision concerns weights or activations, and the preceding action. Its continuous action is rounded to a discrete bitwidth (2–8 bits in the paper’s experiments). The candidate network is evaluated against target accelerator feedback; the resource budget is enforced by reducing bitwidth if needed. After one epoch of quantized fine-tuning, validation accuracy relative to the original model supplies the reward. The paper’s linear quantizer uses a clipping range selected by minimizing KL divergence between original and quantized weight distributions; activation values are clipped to a nonnegative range. Thus HAQ uses layer features and actual/simulated hardware behavior to allocate precision, rather than estimating local Hessian sensitivity. [6]

## Experimental results reported by the papers

All metrics below are **author-reported paper results**, not independent reproductions. Baselines and benchmark conditions differ across papers and should not be treated as head-to-head results unless the paper explicitly compares them.

- **HAWQ / ResNet20, CIFAR-10:** the paper reports 92.22% test accuracy with mixed-precision weights whose lowest layer precision is 2 bits, 4-bit activations, 13.11× weight compression, and 8× activation compression. Its 32-bit baseline is 92.37%; the direct quantization baseline with the same lowest weight and activation bit settings scores 90.34%. The HAWQ paper compares with DNAS and reports similar accuracy with 8× greater activation compression, but that is the authors’ comparison, not a new independent reproduction. [2]
- **HAWQ / ImageNet:** for Inception-V3, the reported result is 75.52% top-1 at 7.57 MB (2-bit minimum mixed-precision weights, 4-bit minimum mixed-precision activations), against a 77.45% 32-bit baseline and 69.76% direct quantization at the same nominal minimum precision. For ResNet50, HAWQ reports 75.48% top-1 at 7.96 MB versus HAQ’s cited 75.30% at 9.22 MB; the HAWQ paper says the models/settings it compares yield HAWQ’s higher accuracy and smaller size. SqueezeNext reaches 68.02% top-1 at 1.09 MB versus 69.38% baseline. These figures come from the HAWQ paper’s own tables and comparisons. [2]
- **HAWQ-V2 / ImageNet:** Inception-V3 scores 75.68% top-1 at 7.57 MB (12.04× weight compression), compared with 77.45% full precision and 69.76% direct quantization in the paper’s table. ResNet50 scores 75.76% at 7.99 MB (12.24× weight compression), compared with 77.39% full precision and 75.30% for HAQ at 9.22 MB. SqueezeNext scores 68.38% at 1.07 MB, compared with a 69.38% baseline and 65.39% direct quantization. [3] [4]
- **HAWQ-V2 / RetinaNet on COCO 2017:** baseline is 35.6 mAP at 145 MB. Direct uniform 4-bit weights and activations yield 31.5 mAP at 18.13 MB; FQN at the same stated 4-bit/18.13 MB setting yields 32.5 mAP. HAWQ-V2 reports 34.1 mAP at 17.90 MB with mixed-precision weights (minimum 3 bits) and uniform 4-bit activations; mixed-precision activations report 34.4 mAP at the same model size and 7.62× activation compression. Uniform 6-bit activation is listed at 34.8 mAP, at lower activation compression. These are the paper’s RetinaNet/COCO results and stated baselines. [4]
- **HAWQ-V2 compute experiment:** the paper reports estimating average Hessian traces for all 54 ResNet50 blocks in 30 minutes with four GPUs, about 33 seconds per block. It reports 50 Hutchinson steps as sufficient in its tested setup and notes convergence as its data sample grows beyond 512. The paper separately says candidate scoring for about 1,000 allocations takes less than one second on one CPU. These are author-reported timings tied to their implementation/setup; hardware model and full end-to-end runtime are not specified in the cited passage. [4]
- **HAQ / ImageNet, MobileNet-V1 and MobileNet-V2:** HAQ evaluates on BISMO edge/cloud FPGA configurations (Zynq-7020 at batch 1 and VU9P at batch 16) and a BitFusion accelerator. The paper’s summary claim is 1.4–1.95× lower latency and 1.9× lower energy than fixed 8-bit quantization with negligible accuracy loss. For example, its BitFusion MobileNet-V1 table compares a 20.08 ms 8-bit baseline with HAQ’s flexible policy at 11.09 ms (70.40% top-1) under a similar accuracy target. A separate energy table reports 31.03 mJ for the 8-bit original and 16.30 mJ for a flexible policy at 70.37% top-1, versus 70.82% for the original. In model-size-constrained ImageNet experiments, the paper reports its flexible policies against Deep Compression; e.g., ResNet-50 at 3-bit-equivalent model-size policy is 75.30% top-1 and 9.22 MB, compared with 75.10% and 9.36 MB for the cited baseline. These measures reflect paper-specified accelerator configurations/objectives, not generic speedups on arbitrary hardware. [5] [6]

## Computational requirements

The original HAWQ paper estimates its top Hessian eigenvalue calculation at roughly 20 gradient back-propagations per block. It acknowledges this as additional compute; the paper’s acknowledgments state that two donated Titan Xp GPUs were partially used, but do not establish that those GPUs are a required minimum. [2]

HAWQ-V2’s reported trace experiment used four GPUs for 30 minutes for 54 ResNet50 blocks. Its Pareto scoring is described as CPU-cheap once traces and quantization perturbations have been computed. Training data and a trained model are required in the described procedure; the authors explicitly identify unavailable training data as an important future-work problem. [4]

HAQ requires a model, quantization/fine-tuning and repeated RL search, plus access to a compatible hardware measurement or simulation environment for the target accelerator. The paper gives the example devices and partial ImageNet exploration setup (100 randomly selected classes for short-term exploration fine-tuning, followed by final full-dataset fine-tuning), but does not report an overall search wall-clock, total number of candidate evaluations, energy cost of search, or a universal hardware minimum. Do not infer a compute budget from the reported inference latency/energy. [6]

## Limitations and caveats

HAWQ’s first version ranks relative sensitivity using only the largest eigenvalue and does not determine exact bitwidths automatically; its authors identify that manual choice and lack of mixed-precision activation analysis as limitations that HAWQ-V2 addresses. Even HAWQ-V2’s Pareto objective is a proxy for post-quantization performance rather than a guarantee. Its theoretical argument relies on assumptions including local convergence, zero gradient, positive-semidefinite Hessian, and a specified perturbation structure; its practical trace estimates are sampled approximations. HAWQ-V2 needs training examples for the described sensitivity computation and may not apply directly when those data cannot be accessed. [2] [4]

HAQ’s learned allocation is intentionally hardware- and objective-specific: its paper shows that policies vary across edge/cloud architectures and latency, energy, or model-size goals. The results therefore cannot be carried over as a universal bit policy or performance guarantee. RL exploration and repeated quantized fine-tuning incur work, but the paper does not provide an overall search-cost figure in the cited material. The paper’s one-epoch reward fine-tuning and 100-class exploration subset are part of its experimental protocol, not proof that one epoch is sufficient for arbitrary tasks. [6]

Mixed precision is useful only when target execution formats and kernels actually support the assigned granularities. HAWQ itself notes that mixed-precision inference on embedded processors is more complicated than uniform precision. HAWQ and HAQ evaluate historical computer-vision tasks/models; the cited results do not establish performance for QML’s Rust stack, language models, different quantizers, or other objectives. [2] [4] [6]

## Licensing and implementation status

The HAWQ and HAQ CVF pages state that the proceedings’ open-access copies are provided for dissemination and that copyright is retained by authors or other rights holders; the arXiv records show arXiv distribution terms, not a blanket permissive reuse license. The HAWQ-V2 arXiv page likewise displays arXiv’s perpetual non-exclusive distribution license. No open-source license for the papers themselves was verified. [1] [2] [4] [5] [6]

The official [HAQ repository](https://github.com/mit-han-lab/haq) identifies its code as MIT-licensed and gives setup details for its PyTorch implementation. The official [HAWQ repository](https://github.com/Zhen-Dong/HAWQ) identifies its software as MIT-licensed and describes a PyTorch quantization library with TVM hardware implementation. However, the HAWQ repository’s displayed experimental tables explicitly refer to HAWQ-V3; those maintainer-provided results must not be attributed to the HAWQ-V2 paper or treated as independent reproduction of HAWQ-V2. Repository licensing does not establish licenses for separately distributed pretrained weights/checkpoints. A distinct permissive license for either project’s weights/checkpoints was not verified. [7] [8]

## Relevance to QML and potential overlap

**Established published method:** Hessian-/trace-weighted layer sensitivity and hardware-feedback-driven mixed-precision allocation are established methods. Any QML work that assigns different precision to model components based on curvature, estimated perturbation impact, activations, hardware cost, or a constrained Pareto objective should be compared against these prior methods. Similarity of high-level goal alone is not sufficient to claim algorithmic identity; the quantizer, sensitivity estimator, granularity, objective, search procedure, calibration/training assumptions, and runtime target must be compared directly. [1] [3] [5]

**QML hypothesis:** The mechanisms may be relevant to a local-first Rust lab if QML experiments allocate representation precision across layers/tensors or trade fidelity against memory, latency, or compute. Possible overlap is strongest if RMQ, MAP, or QMF performs per-layer/per-activation precision assignment or sensitivity-guided compression. If those QML names instead refer to different constructs, the overlap may be only at a broad optimization level. This is a research-comparison hypothesis, not a claim that QML implements or originates HAWQ/HAQ.

**Unknown:** RMQ, MAP, and QMF are not defined in the task materials available for this entry, so exact feature-by-feature overlap cannot be verified. No method here is attributed to QML, and no QML reproduction is claimed. The QML relevance section is a hypothesis for later comparison against the project’s own specifications.
